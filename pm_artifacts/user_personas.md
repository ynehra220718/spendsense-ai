# SpendSense AI — User Personas

*All financial figures are grounded in real 2024–2025 research data. Sources cited per persona.*

## Persona 1: Priya

**"I know I earn well. I just don't know where it all goes."**

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Age | 28 |
| Location | Austin, TX |
| Occupation | Software Engineer (Mid-Level) |
| Annual Income | $95,000 gross / ~$5,900/mo net |
| Monthly Surplus | ~$2,212 |
| Total Debt | $36,000 (student loans $31,200 + CC $4,800) |
| Savings | $28,500 (401K $17,300 + HYSA $11,200) |
| Persona Type | `budget_guilt` |

### Financial Reality
Priya earns above the US median but experiences "money dysmorphia" — a documented phenomenon where high earners feel financially insecure due to lifestyle creep and early-career anxiety (Jenius Bank, 2025). She is below Fidelity's 1x-salary savings benchmark heading into age 30, which drives persistent low-grade financial guilt. Her credit card balance ($4,800 at 21.5% APR) is slightly below the Millennial average of $6,961 (Experian, 2025) — she pays above minimum but never fully clears it.

She has 8 active subscriptions — two of which she hasn't used in 60 days. This is typical: Millennials are the generation most likely to carry 6-11 simultaneous subscriptions (industry research, 2024).

### Behavioral Profile
- **Money script:** *"I should be doing better financially given how much I earn — but somehow there's never enough left over."*
- **Primary bias:** Present bias + lifestyle creep. Spending has expanded proportionally with every raise.
- **Spending triggers:** Work stress → small reward purchases; Instagram social proof; "limited time" urgency signals; burnout-driven self-care spending.
- **Financial anxiety:** High. 58% of Millennials experience financial anxiety 3+ days/week (LifeStance, 2025). Priya is in this group — she checks her bank balance anxiously after larger purchases.

### What She Needs from SpendSense
A **clear go/no-go signal** with honest rationale she can trust. She doesn't need to be told to save more — she already knows. She needs someone (or something) to confirm her instinct when a purchase is actually fine, and to name the specific financial tension when it's not. The coaching should feel like a smart friend who knows her finances, not a judgmental budget app.

### SpendSense Demo Scenario
**Scenario A:** Instant Pot Duo 7-in-1 · $280
Expected verdict: **CAUTION** — purchase is within budget but conflicts with credit card payoff goal. Coaching should name the present bias ("you've been thinking about this for weeks") and offer the historical low ($219.99 in Nov 2025) as a concrete wait target.

## Persona 2: Marcus

**"I've done the research. I just need someone to tell me if the numbers actually work."**

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Age | 34 |
| Location | Columbus, OH |
| Occupation | Operations Manager |
| Annual Income | $72,000 gross / ~$4,680/mo net |
| Monthly Surplus | ~$277 |
| Total Debt | $29,600 (auto $18,800 + CC $6,800 + BNPL $4,000) |
| Savings | $52,000 (401K $48,500 + liquid $3,500) |
| Persona Type | `comparison_shopper` |

### Financial Reality
Marcus's budget looks adequate on paper but his monthly surplus is only $277 after a car payment, healthcare, and rent in Columbus. This is not uncommon: research shows that 36% of Americans earning $75K-$100K live paycheck to paycheck (Debt.org, 2024).

His most significant hidden vulnerability is **off-balance-sheet BNPL debt**: $4,000 across three active plans (Affirm ×2, Klarna ×1). This is a documented blind spot among analytical spenders who use BNPL to "optimize" large purchases — they treat installments as cash flow management, not debt. 44% of Gen Z used BNPL in 2024 (Motley Fool); Marcus has adopted this pattern despite being older.

His 401K ($48,500) is below the Empower median of $80,000 for the 30-39 cohort — a gap he is aware of and anxious about.

### Behavioral Profile
- **Money script:** *"If I research long enough, I'll find the objectively right purchase — and then I deserve it."*
- **Primary bias:** Research justification trap + BNPL blind spot. Extended research creates sunk cost that makes the purchase feel inevitable. BNPL removes sticker shock.
- **Spending triggers:** Finding a "deal" after research; BNPL availability; productivity-framed purchases ("I need this for work"); competitive benchmarking against peers.
- **Financial anxiety:** Medium. Marcus is aware his budget is tight but frames it as temporary. His anxiety concentrates on retirement adequacy, not day-to-day spending.

### What He Needs from SpendSense
**Data-driven alternative analysis.** Marcus respects research and numbers. He needs SpendSense to meet him at his analytical level — show him the price history, name the BNPL debt he's discounting, and give him three concrete alternatives ranked by value. He won't respond to emotional appeals; he responds to better data.

### SpendSense Demo Scenario
**Scenario B:** Dell XPS 15 Laptop · $1,200
Expected verdict: **NOT_RECOMMENDED** — at $277/mo surplus with $4K BNPL already active, this triggers Deal Hunter. Coach should name the "productivity purchase rationalization" pattern specifically. Alternatives: refurbished XPS 15 (Amazon Renewed), wait for Dell Education sale, or rent via company expense policy.

## Persona 3: Destiny

**"I'm trying to be better with money. Some days are harder than others."**

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Age | 25 |
| Location | Atlanta, GA |
| Occupation | Program Coordinator, Nonprofit (Youth Education) |
| Annual Income | $48,000 gross / ~$3,280/mo net |
| Monthly Surplus | ~$834 (inconsistent month-to-month) |
| Total Debt | $42,300 (student loans $34,500 + BNPL $1,800 + CC $2,800 + medical $3,200) |
| Savings | $1,200 (HYSA opened 4 months ago) |
| Persona Type | `rebuilding_finances` |

### Financial Reality
Destiny's budget is structurally fragile. Her $834 monthly surplus looks reasonable but evaporates under any unexpected expense — 59% of Americans cannot cover a $1,000 emergency from savings (Bankrate, 2025), and Destiny is solidly in this group. Her emergency fund ($1,200) covers approximately 2 weeks of expenses.

Her student loan payment is only $98/mo because she's enrolled in Income-Driven Repayment (IDR) and pursuing Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF). This is financially smart but psychologically reduces urgency — the real balance ($34,500) feels abstract.

She carries $3,200 in medical debt from an ER visit at 23 while uninsured between jobs. 18% of adults aged 18-29 have borrowed money for healthcare (Federal Reserve SHED, 2024). This isn't financial irresponsibility — it's a structural gap in coverage that affected her early career.

Her 2 active BNPL plans (Afterpay clothing $480, Affirm phone accessories $1,320) represent a spending pattern documented across 44% of Gen Z (Motley Fool, 2025). Notably, 25% of Gen Z were late on a BNPL payment in 2024 — Destiny has missed one payment.

### Behavioral Profile
- **Money script:** *"I grew up without much money and I'm afraid I'll always be bad at this — but I'm trying to change that."*
- **Primary pattern:** Stress spending as emotional release + BNPL normalizes larger purchases. 30% of Gen Z treat themselves to purchases when worried about money (Fortune, 2024). Destiny is actively working against this pattern.
- **Signs of progress:** Audited and cut 4 subscriptions 3 months ago. Opened HYSA. Enrolled in IDR. These are real behavioral wins that matter to her financial identity.
- **Spending triggers:** TikTok Shop and Instagram ads (primary); BNPL removing friction; "doom spending" during stressful news cycles (documented Gen Z coping mechanism, Fortune 2024); social pull from peers.
- **Financial anxiety:** High. 62% of Gen Z stressed 3+ days/week about money (LifeStance, 2025). Destiny's anxiety is compounded by financial identity fragility — she is actively trying to shift her self-concept from "bad with money" to "someone who is learning."

### What She Needs from SpendSense
**Non-judgmental coaching that celebrates progress.** Destiny is in financial transition. She knows the numbers aren't great — she doesn't need a lecture. She needs a system that acknowledges her genuine progress, names her spending triggers without shame, and redirects with warmth. The framing must always be "not right now" not "you can't." One bad coaching message could undermine the fragile financial confidence she is building.

### SpendSense Demo Scenario
**Scenario C:** Nike Air Max 270 Sneakers · $95
Expected verdict: **CAUTION to NOT_RECOMMENDED** — BNPL still active ($1,800 outstanding), emergency fund critically low (12% of 3-month target). Likely a TikTok-triggered impulse. Coach should validate the desire, gently note the BNPL pattern, and redirect to her $2,500 emergency fund milestone — framing the $95 saved as 7.7% of the way there.
